What is Aggregate Planning?

Aggregate planning is the process of determining the optimal production, workforce, and inventory levels over a medium-term planning horizon (typically 3 to 18 months), treating all products as a single aggregate unit (e.g., tons of output, standard hours of work).

The goal is to satisfy forecasted demand at minimum cost by appropriately combining the available capacity adjustment levers:

  • Workforce — hiring, firing, and temporarily laying off workers
  • Production rate — overtime, undertime, or shift changes
  • Inventory — building stock in advance of peak demand
  • Backorders — allowing demand to be satisfied in a later period
  • Subcontracting — outsourcing production to external suppliers
Scope: Aggregate planning bridges long-range capacity planning (facilities, major equipment) and short-range scheduling (job sequencing, lot sizing). It is the medium-range layer of the manufacturing planning and control hierarchy.

Total Cost Function

$$\text{Total Cost} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \Bigl[ c_R P_t + c_H H_t + c_F F_t + c_I \max(I_t,\,0) + c_B \max(-I_t,\,0) + c_{OT} O_t + c_{SC} S_t + c_{LS} L_t \Bigr]$$
The $c_{LS} L_t$ term is only active under the lost-sales shortage policy; under backorders $L_t = 0$, and under no-shortages both $L_t = 0$ and $I_t^- = 0$ (the solver enforces feasibility by preventing negative inventory).
Inventory splitting: Because $I_t$ can be negative (backorder), costs are applied asymmetrically: $c_I$ on the positive part and $c_B$ on the absolute value of the negative part. In LP formulations this is handled by introducing $I_t^+ = \max(I_t,0)$ and $I_t^- = \max(-I_t,0)$, so $I_t = I_t^+ - I_t^-$.

Chase Demand Strategy

The chase strategy adjusts the workforce each period so that production exactly matches the demand forecast. Inventory is kept at (approximately) zero, and all variability is absorbed by hiring and firing.

Core Idea

$$P_t = D_t \qquad \forall\, t$$

Workforce Calculation

$$W_t = \frac{D_t}{\rho}$$

where $\rho$ is the regular-time productivity (units per worker per period).

Hiring and Firing

$$H_t = \max\bigl(0,\; W_t - W_{t-1}\bigr)$$ $$F_t = \max\bigl(0,\; W_{t-1} - W_t\bigr)$$

Inventory

$$I_t = I_{t-1} + P_t - D_t = I_{t-1} \approx 0 \quad (\text{with } I_0 \text{ consumed first})$$

Total Cost

$$C_{\text{Chase}} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \bigl(c_R D_t + c_H H_t + c_F F_t\bigr)$$
Key trade-off: Inventory cost is minimised, but hiring & firing costs can be very high when demand is volatile. This strategy places the burden of uncertainty on the workforce, which may also raise labour relations concerns.

📌 Intuition

Imagine demand doubles in June relative to May. Under the chase strategy, the workforce must nearly double — all workers above the May level must be hired at cost $c_H$ each. If demand then drops back in July, those same workers are fired at cost $c_F$. High demand variability = high workforce churn = high cost under this strategy.

4

Level Production Strategy

The level strategy keeps the production rate constant across all periods. The workforce remains stable; demand variability is absorbed by inventory (excess production is stocked) and backorders (stockouts are filled in future periods).

Constant Production Rate

$$\bar{P} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T} D_t}{T}$$

Workforce Level

$$\bar{W} = \frac{\bar{P}}{\rho}$$

Inventory Evolution

$$I_t = I_{t-1} + \bar{P} - D_t \qquad t = 1,\ldots,T$$

Total Cost

$$C_{\text{Level}} = c_H \max(0, \bar{W} - W_0) + c_F \max(0, W_0 - \bar{W}) + c_R \cdot T \cdot \bar{P} + c_I \sum_{t: I_t>0} I_t + c_B \sum_{t: I_t<0} |I_t|$$
Key trade-off: Hiring and firing costs are minimised (at most one adjustment at the start of the horizon), but inventory holding and backorder costs rise when demand is seasonal. This strategy favours scenarios where $c_H + c_F \gg c_I$.
Feasibility condition: For the level strategy to avoid all backorders (if backorders are not acceptable), the cumulative production must always exceed cumulative demand: $$I_0 + n \cdot \bar{P} \geq \sum_{t=1}^{n} D_t \qquad \forall\, n = 1,\ldots,T$$ If this condition fails, a higher production rate (larger workforce) or backorders must be accepted.
5

Mixed / Hybrid Strategy

A mixed strategy does not restrict itself to pure chase or pure level behaviour. The planner simultaneously uses multiple levers — partial workforce changes, overtime, subcontracting, and some inventory — to minimise total cost.

Formulation

$$P_t = \underbrace{W_t \cdot \rho}_{\text{Regular-time}} + \underbrace{O_t}_{\text{Overtime}} + \underbrace{S_t}_{\text{Subcontract}}$$
$$I_t = I_{t-1} + P_t - D_t$$
$$W_t = W_{t-1} + H_t - F_t$$

Practical Approach

In practice a "target workforce" $W^*$ is chosen (often the average): $$W^* = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{D_t}{\rho}$$ The workforce is held at $W^*$ each period, and remaining gaps are filled first with overtime and then with subcontracting:

$$\text{Gap}_t = \max\bigl(0,\; D_t - I_{t-1} - W^* \cdot \rho\bigr)$$ $$O_t = \min\bigl(\text{Gap}_t,\; \alpha \cdot W^* \cdot \rho\bigr)$$ $$S_t = \min\bigl(\text{Gap}_t - O_t,\; \bar{S}\bigr)$$
Why mixed? When $c_H, c_F$ are moderate (not extreme) and overtime/subcontract costs are also moderate, no pure strategy dominates. The optimal split depends on the specific cost ratios — which is exactly why the LP model is the correct tool to find it.

Linear Programming Model

The LP model finds the globally optimal aggregate plan by formulating the problem as a linear program. Unlike heuristic strategies, LP guarantees the minimum-cost solution subject to all stated constraints.

6.1 Decision Variables

For each period $t = 1, \ldots, T$:

VariableMeaningDomain
$P_t$Total production (RT + OT + Sub)$\geq 0$
$W_t$Workforce level$\geq 0$
$H_t$Workers hired$\geq 0$
$F_t$Workers fired$\geq 0$
$I_t^+$Positive inventory (holding)$\geq 0$
$I_t^-$Negative inventory (backorder)$\geq 0$
$O_t$Overtime units$\geq 0$
$S_t$Subcontracted units$\geq 0$
$L_t$Lost sales in period $t$ (unmet demand dropped forever)$\geq 0$, policy-dependent
The inventory is split into $I_t^+$ (excess stock) and $I_t^-$ (backorder quantity) so that different costs ($c_I$ and $c_B$) can be applied to each part within the LP framework: $I_t = I_t^+ - I_t^-$.

6.2 Objective Function

$$\min\; Z = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \Bigl[ \frac{c_R}{\rho}\, P_t \;+\; c_H H_t \;+\; c_F F_t \;+\; c_I I_t^+ \;+\; c_B I_t^- \;+\; c_{OT} O_t \;+\; c_{SC} S_t \Bigr] \tag{OF}$$

6.3 Constraints

Inventory Balance (demand satisfaction)
$$I_t^+ - I_t^- = I_{t-1}^+ - I_{t-1}^- + P_t - D_t \qquad \forall\, t \tag{C1}$$

Equivalently, with $I_0 = I_0^+ $ (given initial inventory): all demand must be met, either from production or from inventory, with possible backorders.

Workforce Balance
$$W_t = W_{t-1} + H_t - F_t \qquad \forall\, t \tag{C2}$$

The workforce in period $t$ equals last period's workforce plus hires minus fires.

Regular-Time Capacity
$$P_t - O_t - S_t \leq W_t \cdot \rho \qquad \forall\, t \tag{C3}$$

Regular-time production (total production minus overtime and subcontract) cannot exceed workforce capacity.

Overtime Capacity
$$O_t \leq \alpha \cdot W_t \cdot \rho \qquad \forall\, t \tag{C4}$$
Subcontracting Capacity
$$S_t \leq \bar{S} \qquad \forall\, t \tag{C5}$$
Maximum Workforce
$$W_t \leq \bar{W} \qquad \forall\, t \tag{C6}$$
Non-negativity
$$P_t,\, W_t,\, H_t,\, F_t,\, I_t^+,\, I_t^-,\, O_t,\, S_t \geq 0 \qquad \forall\, t \tag{C7}$$

6.4 Complete Compact Formulation

$$\begin{aligned} \min\; & \sum_{t=1}^{T} \Bigl[\tfrac{c_R}{\rho} P_t + c_H H_t + c_F F_t + c_I I_t^+ + c_B I_t^- + c_{OT} O_t + c_{SC} S_t\Bigr] \\[6pt] \text{s.t.}\quad & I_t^+ - I_t^- = I_{t-1}^+ - I_{t-1}^- + P_t - D_t, \quad &&\forall\, t \\ & W_t = W_{t-1} + H_t - F_t, \quad &&\forall\, t \\ & P_t - O_t - S_t \leq W_t \cdot \rho, \quad &&\forall\, t \\ & O_t \leq \alpha\, W_t \cdot \rho, \quad &&\forall\, t \\ & S_t \leq \bar{S}, \quad &&\forall\, t \\ & W_t \leq \bar{W}, \quad &&\forall\, t \\ & P_t,\, W_t,\, H_t,\, F_t,\, I_t^+,\, I_t^-,\, O_t,\, S_t \geq 0, \quad &&\forall\, t \end{aligned}$$

6.5 Shadow Prices (Dual Variables)

Each equality constraint has an associated dual variable (shadow price) $\mu_t$. The shadow price of the inventory balance constraint (C1) in period $t$ represents:

$$\mu_t = \frac{\partial Z^*}{\partial D_t}$$

That is, $\mu_t$ is the marginal increase in the optimal total cost when demand in period $t$ increases by one unit, holding all other parameters fixed.

Shadow Price ValueInterpretation
$\mu_t = c_I$ One extra unit of demand in period $t$ costs exactly one extra period of holding — the binding constraint is inventory capacity.
$\mu_t = c_R / \rho$ Extra demand is met by regular-time production — no bottleneck elsewhere.
$\mu_t = c_{OT}$ Extra demand forces overtime — workforce capacity is binding.
$\mu_t > c_{OT}$ Multiple constraints are simultaneously binding; extra demand is very costly.
Pedagogical value: Shadow prices reveal which constraints are actually limiting production in each period, and what it would cost to relax them (e.g., hiring one more worker, allowing 5% more overtime). This is powerful for what-if analysis in class.

6.7 Implementation Note

In the dashboard, the LP is solved by SciPy's HiGHS solver (scipy.optimize.linprog with method='highs'), a state-of-the-art open-source LP/MIP solver. The base model has $9T$ variables ($x, h, f, w, I^+, I^-, O, S, L$ per period) and $2T$ equality + $4T$ inequality constraints — typically solved in milliseconds for $T \leq 24$.

When the Integer Variables option is active, the solver switches to scipy.optimize.milp (HiGHS MIP backend) with an integrality vector that marks the chosen variable slices as integers. Shadow prices are not available in MIP mode.

Transportation Method

The transportation method (also called the tabular LP method) casts the aggregate planning problem as a classical transportation problem — one of the most well-studied problems in operations research. This formulation can be solved by hand using a structured tableau and is particularly useful for illustrating the cost trade-off between producing early (and holding inventory) versus producing late.

7.1 The Core Idea: Production as Shipment

Think of each potential production source (regular-time in period 1, overtime in period 1, etc.) as a warehouse with a certain supply capacity. Each period's demand is a customer that must receive exactly $D_t$ units. The "cost of shipping" one unit from source $i$ to demand period $j$ is the sum of the production cost and any holding cost incurred while the unit waits in inventory between periods $i$ and $j$.

The objective is to find the minimum-cost allocation of production from all sources to all demand periods, subject to:

  • Supply constraints: each source cannot ship more than its capacity;
  • Demand constraints: each demand period must receive exactly $D_t$ units.
Key insight: Because production in period $i$ can only serve demand in periods $j \geq i$ without incurring backorders, the cost matrix is upper-triangular (cells below the diagonal are either forbidden or penalised by the backorder cost $c_B$). The method automatically finds the optimal timing of production across the horizon.

7.2 Tableau Structure

A transportation tableau for $T$ periods with regular-time (RT), overtime (OT), and subcontracting (SC) has the following layout:

  • Rows — one per supply source:
    • Row 0: Initial inventory $I_0$ (already on hand, zero production cost);
    • Rows $1 \ldots T$: Regular-time production in each period (capacity $R_t$, cost $c_R$);
    • Rows $T+1 \ldots 2T$: Overtime production in each period (capacity $O_t^{\max}$, cost $c_{OT}$);
    • Rows $2T+1 \ldots 3T$: Subcontracting in each period (capacity $\bar{S}$, cost $c_{SC}$).
  • Columns — one per demand period $t = 1, \ldots, T$, with requirement $D_t$.
  • Cell $(s,\,i \to j)$ — the unit cost of producing one unit via source $s$ in period $i$ to satisfy demand in period $j$.
  • Dummy column — if total supply exceeds total demand, a dummy column with zero cost absorbs the surplus (unused capacity).

7.3 Cell Cost Formulas

Normal delivery — unit produced in period $i$, consumed in period $j \geq i$:

$$\text{cost}(s,\,i \to j) = c_s + c_I \cdot (j - i), \qquad j \geq i$$

where $c_s \in \{c_R,\, c_{OT},\, c_{SC}\}$ and $c_I \cdot (j-i)$ is the holding cost for keeping the unit in stock for $j - i$ periods.

Backorder — unit produced in period $i$, but demand was in period $j < i$:

$$\text{cost}(s,\,i \to j) = c_s + c_B \cdot (i - j), \qquad j < i$$

In most formulations backorders are forbidden; those cells are set to a large penalty $M$ (sometimes written $\infty$) to force the solver to avoid them.

Initial inventory row ($I_0$, produced in period 0):

$$\text{cost}(I_0 \to j) = c_I \cdot j, \qquad j = 1, \ldots, T$$

(These units are already in stock; the only relevant cost is the holding cost from period 0 through period $j$.)

7.4 Mathematical Formulation

Let $x_{ij} \geq 0$ be the number of units allocated from source row $i$ to demand period $j$. The problem is:

$$\min \sum_{i} \sum_{j} c_{ij}\, x_{ij}$$

Subject to:

$$\sum_{j} x_{ij} \leq \text{supply}_i \qquad \forall\, i \quad \text{(supply capacity)}$$ $$\sum_{i} x_{ij} = D_j \qquad \forall\, j \quad \text{(demand satisfaction)}$$ $$x_{ij} \geq 0 \qquad \forall\, i, j$$

This is a standard LP with a totally unimodular constraint matrix, guaranteeing integer optimal solutions when all supplies and demands are integers.

7.5 Solution Methods

Step 1 — Northwest Corner Method (Initial Feasible Solution)

The Northwest Corner method builds an initial feasible allocation by working from the top-left (northwest) cell of the tableau to the bottom-right:

  1. Start at cell $(1,1)$ — the top-left corner.
  2. Allocate as many units as possible: $x_{ij} = \min(\text{remaining supply}_i,\;\text{remaining demand}_j)$.
  3. If supply of row $i$ is exhausted, move down one row.
  4. If demand of column $j$ is satisfied, move right one column.
  5. Repeat until all demand is satisfied.
Note: The Northwest Corner method ignores costs entirely — it produces a feasible but generally sub-optimal solution used only as a starting point for the improvement phase.

Step 2 — Stepping-Stone Method (Optimality Improvement)

Once an initial feasible solution exists, check whether any empty (unallocated) cell could reduce total cost if some units were re-routed through it:

  1. For each empty cell $(i^*, j^*)$, find a closed loop connecting $(i^*, j^*)$ back to itself, alternating between occupied cells in the same row and column.
  2. Compute the loop cost change: $$\Delta_{i^*j^*} = c_{i^*j^*} - c_{i^*j_1} + c_{i_1 j_1} - c_{i_1 j^*} + \cdots$$ (signs alternate: $+$ for the entering cell and $-$ for the departing cells).
  3. If $\Delta_{i^*j^*} < 0$, re-routing units through that cell reduces cost — perform the reallocation (shift as many units as the most restrictive $-$ cell allows).
  4. Repeat until $\Delta_{ij} \geq 0$ for all empty cells → optimal solution reached.

Alternative: MODI Method (Modified Distribution)

The MODI (Modified Distribution) method computes the same loop cost changes more efficiently by assigning dual variables $u_i$ (row) and $v_j$ (column) such that $u_i + v_j = c_{ij}$ for all occupied cells. The reduced cost of each empty cell is then simply $\bar{c}_{ij} = c_{ij} - u_i - v_j$. If any $\bar{c}_{ij} < 0$, that cell enters the basis.

7.7 Balancing the Tableau (Supply ≠ Demand)

Transportation problems require total supply = total demand. In aggregate planning, supply usually exceeds demand (there is unused capacity). Balance is achieved by adding a dummy demand column with:

  • Requirement = Total Supply − Total Demand;
  • Cost = $0 for all cells (unused capacity has no cost).

Any allocation to the dummy column represents idle capacity — production that could have occurred but did not need to.

Trial-and-Error / Spreadsheet Method

The trial-and-error method is not an algorithm — it is a structured what-if analysis. The planner manually specifies a feasible production plan, the spreadsheet (or dashboard) calculates total cost, and the planner iterates by adjusting the plan to reduce cost.

Cost Dominance Rule

$$C_{\text{LP}}^* \;\leq\; C_{\text{Transport}}^* \;\leq\; \min\bigl(C_{\text{Chase}},\, C_{\text{Level}},\, C_{\text{Mixed}}\bigr)$$

The LP solution always dominates, because the LP feasible region contains the solutions producible by all other strategies as special cases.

Shortage Policies

A shortage policy specifies what happens when available supply (production plus beginning inventory) is insufficient to meet demand in a given period. The dashboard supports three mutually exclusive policies.

10.1 Backorders (default)

Unmet demand is deferred to a future period. Customers wait; the firm fulfils the order later at a backorder penalty cost $c_B$ per unit per period late. Inventory becomes negative during the backlog period.

$$I_t = I_{t-1} + P_t - D_t \quad (\text{can be} < 0)$$
FeatureValue
Demand satisfied?Yes — eventually (late)
$I_t^-$ (backorder stock)$\geq 0$, free
$L_t$ (lost sales)$= 0$ (fixed)
Service level100% (fill rate over time)
Revenue impactPenalty cost only; revenue eventually earned
Best for: Make-to-order environments; loyal customers willing to wait; when the cost of losing a sale exceeds holding extra inventory.

10.2 No Shortages

Negative inventory is forbidden. The solver forces $I_t \geq 0$ at all times by setting $I_t^- = 0$ and $L_t = 0$ in the bounds. If demand cannot be met from production + inventory, the model is infeasible and a warning is displayed.

$$I_t \geq 0 \quad \forall\, t$$
Pedagogical use: This is the most restrictive policy. It forces the solver to build safety stock in advance of demand peaks, clearly illustrating the cost of service-level guarantees.

10.3 Lost Sales

Unmet demand is dropped permanently — there is no backlog. The firm incurs a lost-sales penalty $c_{LS}$ per unit not delivered, but does not carry negative inventory. A new decision variable $L_t \geq 0$ enters the model.

Modified Inventory Balance

$$I_{t}^+ - I_{t}^- + L_t + P_t + O_t + S_t = D_t + I_{t-1}^+ - I_{t-1}^-$$

With $I_t^- = 0$ enforced (no backlog), this reduces to:

$$I_t^+ + L_t = D_t - I_{t-1}^+ + \text{(supply gaps)}$$

Modified Objective

$$\min\; Z = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \Bigl[ \tfrac{c_R}{\rho} P_t + c_H H_t + c_F F_t + c_I I_t^+ + c_{OT} O_t + c_{SC} S_t + c_{LS} L_t \Bigr]$$
FeatureValue
Demand satisfied?Partially — lost units forfeited
$I_t^-$ (backorder)$= 0$ (forbidden)
$L_t$ (lost sales)$\geq 0$, free to optimise
Service level$< 100\%$ when capacity constrained
Revenue impactLost revenue + penalty $c_{LS}$
Best for: Perishable goods (food, tickets); environments where customers will switch to a competitor rather than wait; spot-market scenarios. $c_{LS}$ should reflect lost contribution margin plus potential reputational cost.

Integer Variables & MILP Extension

The standard LP aggregate planning model treats all decision variables as continuous — workforce levels, hired/fired counts, and production units can all be fractional. In practice, workers are indivisible and production units may need to be whole numbers. This section describes the Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) extension.

11.1 Why Integer Variables Matter

  • Workforce: You cannot hire 2.7 workers. Rounding the LP solution may slightly violate constraints or change the cost in unexpected ways.
  • Production: For discrete products (e.g., machines, large assemblies), fractional production quantities are physically meaningless.
  • Pedagogical value: Comparing the LP relaxation to the MILP solution demonstrates the integrality gap — the extra cost of requiring whole numbers.

11.2 MILP Formulation

The MILP extends the LP by adding integrality constraints on selected variable groups:

OptionVariables forced to $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$
Integer Workforce $H_t,\; F_t,\; W_t$ for all $t$
Integer Production $P_t^{RT},\; O_t,\; S_t$ for all $t$
Both All of the above

Formally, appending to the LP:

$$H_t, F_t, W_t \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \quad \text{(if integer workforce)} \tag{C8}$$ $$P_t^{RT}, O_t, S_t \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \quad \text{(if integer production)} \tag{C9}$$

Inventory split variables $I_t^+$, $I_t^-$, and lost-sales $L_t$ remain continuous even in MILP mode — inventory levels are typically measured in aggregate units and need not be integers.

11.3 Integrality Gap

The integrality gap is the difference between the optimal LP (relaxed) cost and the MILP (integer) cost:

$$\text{Gap} = Z^*_{\text{MILP}} - Z^*_{\text{LP}} \geq 0$$

The gap depends on the problem structure. For workforce variables, the gap is often small (rounding 2.7 to 3 workers slightly increases cost). For production variables with large unit costs, the gap can be significant.

11.4 Heuristic Approximation (non-LP strategies)

For Chase, Level, and Mixed strategies, integrality is enforced by rounding each period's computed values to the nearest integer after the greedy/heuristic calculation. This is an approximation — the rounded solution may not minimise cost among all integer-feasible plans, but it is consistent with the spirit of the heuristic.

Practical advice: Run the MILP first for a true lower bound, then compare with rounded heuristics to assess how much is lost by the heuristic approach. Large integrality gaps suggest the LP relaxation is not a tight bound and MILP is worth the extra computation.

11.5 Solver

The dashboard uses scipy.optimize.milp (HiGHS branch-and-cut backend) when any integer constraint is active. All constraints are expressed via LinearConstraint and Bounds objects with an integrality vector ($0 =$ continuous, $1 =$ integer). Shadow prices are not available in MIP mode (dual variables are only defined for LP relaxations).
